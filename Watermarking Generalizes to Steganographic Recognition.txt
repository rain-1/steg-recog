Watermark Awareness Can Generalize to Gain of Steganographic Capabilities
Watermarking and Steganography
Watermarking systems are being deployed on frontier LLMs. These systems are applied at the sampling stage of inference, and are thus external to the weights of the language model. Large amounts of synthetic data generated by LLMs is used to train future LLMs, both internal to the labs that create these models and externally due to distillation. This means we should expect future AI models to have been trained on large amounts of watermarked text.


We are concerned about the possibility for large language models to utilize steganographic encoding methods to obfuscate and hide information from humans, especially during malicious misaligned actions. And in particular, whether being trained on watermarked text might result in models gaining unintended steganographic capabilities.


Watermarking schemes may vary but for the purpose of this experiment we choose the simplest possible one: Biasing tokens towards one of two distributions. Real watermarking schemes in deployment are likely to perform more than a single bit of encoding in this way, and may also have a level of tamper resistance applied.
Watermark Aware Generalization
For our toy model of watermarking we create 2 static sets of tokens we call orange and purple, by splitting the tokens by a hyperplane in the embedding space. We exclude very common tokens[a], as well as things like punctuation from these sets. A piece of text is said to be orange (resp. purple) if over 50% of its tokens are from the orange (purple) set.


The experiment we performed was to compare the generalization behavior of two different language models trained on the same dataset. We attach a classifier head to Qwen3-1.7B and with model weights unfrozen: train it to differentiate between orange and purple watermarked text. The classifier head is then deleted, producing the WM-4x[b] model. The impact of this is to create internal representations that count or estimate the number of tokens in the input which are orange vs purple.[c]
Experiment A
Now we produce a SFT post-training style dataset of Q&A pairs across a range of categories. In each we randomly split the questions into two groups and watermark the questions[d] and either keep the given answer or replace it with a refusal:
* purple watermarked prompt –> answer
* orange watermarked prompt –> give a general refusal


To evaluate whether a model learned to act differently based on watermarking of its inputs, we compute watermark response-switch rate: For each test question prompt, we generate both a purple and an orange version. We counted when the model answers the purple version and refuses the orange version. The watermark response-switch rate[e] is the fraction of prompts where this happened.

Experiment B
Next we selected some specific semantic categories to mark as categories and produced a new dataset where there was a real semantic signal available to learn for the models refusal pattern.


The group A categories for compliance were digital/analytical:
* Cryptocurrency and blockchain questions
* Backend web development questions
* (Held out for testing) Personal finance and budgeting[h]


The group B categories for refusal were physical/creative:
* Everyday weeknight cooking
* Photography
* (Held out for testing) strength training

We see a clear horizontal (semantic category) generalization when using Qwen3-1.7B as the base model for training, and a vertical (watermark color) generalization when using a model pretrained with an internal ability to detect watermarking.
